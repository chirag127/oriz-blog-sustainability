The most sustainable object is the one you already own, kept working. No new
materials mined, no factory run, no shipping, no packaging. Repair beats
recycling, because recycling still melts things down and remakes them; repair
skips all of that. Yet most of us throw away things that a ten-minute fix would
save, because we were never taught that fixing is an option.

This is a starting guide for reluctant fixers.

## Why we replace things that still work

Three forces push us toward the bin:

- **Design**: glued seams, non-removable batteries, and proprietary screws make
  products hard to open on purpose. This is the fight the right-to-repair
  movement is about, and it is slowly being won.
- **Price**: a new one is often cheap enough that fixing feels like effort for
  no reward. But cheap-and-replaced repeatedly costs more than one good item
  maintained, and it externalises the waste onto everyone.
- **Fear**: most people believe they will break it worse. This is the real
  barrier, and it is the easiest to remove.

## Start where the risk is lowest

You do not learn to repair on your laptop. You learn on things that are already
half-broken, where you cannot make it much worse.

- **Clothes** are the perfect entry point. A popped button, a split seam, a
  small hole. Thread and a needle cost almost nothing, and a wonky first stitch
  is invisible from a metre away. Visible mending, where you make the repair a
  feature with contrasting thread, removes the pressure to be perfect entirely.
- **Furniture**: a wobbly chair is usually a loose screw or dried-out joint, not
  a dead chair. Tighten first, re-glue second.
- **Small electricals**: a lamp that stopped working is often a dead bulb, a
  loose plug wire, or a blown fuse, all of which are safe and simple with the
  power off.

Win on something small and cheap. Confidence is the actual skill; technique
follows.

## The three questions before you bin anything

1. **What actually failed?** "It stopped working" is not a diagnosis. A blender
   that died might have a jammed motor, a broken switch, or just a tripped
   thermal cutout that resets after ten minutes. Search the model number plus
   the symptom before you decide it is dead.
2. **Is the part replaceable?** Batteries, cables, filters, seals, buttons, and
   fuses are usually cheap and standard. If the broken part is one of these, the
   repair is likely trivial.
3. **Has someone fixed this exact thing?** They have. There is a video or a
   forum thread for nearly every consumer product ever made. iFixit alone has
   free guides for thousands of devices, with part suppliers linked.

## Tools worth owning

You can fix a surprising amount with very little:

- A **precision screwdriver set** with swappable bits, including the odd
  security shapes manufacturers use to keep you out.
- A **sewing kit**: needles, a few thread colours, and scissors.
- **Sugru or a good glue** for the "it just needs to hold" jobs.
- A **multimeter** once you are comfortable, to tell a dead component from a
  dead connection.

That is under the price of a single mid-range appliance, and it will save you
many.

## When replacing is the right call

Repair is not a religion. Replace when a device is genuinely unsafe, when the
repair costs more than a used replacement and the part keeps failing, or when a
much more efficient model saves real energy over years, as with a decades-old
fridge. The honest answer is sometimes "let it go." The point is to make that a
decision you reach, not a reflex you skip to.

Pick one broken thing this week. Diagnose it before you judge it. Fix the
easiest thing you own, badly, and notice that the world did not end. That is the
whole practice.
