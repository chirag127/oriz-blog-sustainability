"Eco-friendly." "Natural." "Made with recycled materials." "Carbon neutral."
These phrases are on everything now, and most of them mean nothing on their own.
The point of this post is not to make you cynical. It is to give you a small set
of questions that separate a genuine environmental improvement from a sticker
designed to make you feel better while you pay more.

## Vague words are a warning, not a promise

"Natural," "green," "eco," and "non-toxic" have no legal definition in most
places. Crude oil is natural. Arsenic is natural. A claim that cannot be
measured cannot be checked, and a claim that cannot be checked is marketing.

The tell is specificity. Compare:

- **Vague**: "Made with eco-friendly materials."
- **Specific**: "Made from 60% post-consumer recycled polyester, certified by
  the Global Recycled Standard."

The second one names a number, a source, and a body you can look up. The first
one names a feeling. When a claim gets more specific under scrutiny, it is
probably real. When it stays fuzzy, it probably is not there.

## Ask about the whole life, not one stage

A product can be greenwashed by drawing your eye to one good stage and hiding a
worse one.

- A "biodegradable" cup that only breaks down in an industrial composter you do
  not have access to will sit in landfill like any other.
- A "plastic-free" product wrapped in three layers of foil and card may have a
  bigger footprint than the plastic it replaced.
- An electric gadget marketed as "clean" is only as clean as the grid charging
  it and the mine that supplied its battery.

Ask: made from what, used how, and what happens at the end? A real
improvement holds up across all three. A greenwashed one only holds up in the
photo.

## Watch for the single-issue swap

The most common trick is fixing one visible problem while ignoring the bigger
invisible one. Paper bags feel greener than plastic, but take far more energy
and water to produce, so they only win if you reuse them many times. A cotton
tote is worse than a plastic bag until you have used it dozens or hundreds of
times. The greenest option is almost always the thing you already own, used
until it wears out. New rarely beats reuse, whatever the label says.

## Trust certifications, but know which

Independent, third-party certifications are the closest thing to a shortcut.
They are not perfect, but they mean someone other than the seller checked.
A few worth recognising:

- **Energy Star** and equivalents for appliance efficiency.
- **FSC** for wood and paper from responsibly managed forests.
- **Fairtrade** for supply-chain conditions.
- **B Corp** for company-wide accountability.

A logo the brand invented for itself is not a certification. If the "seal" only
appears on that one company's products, it is decoration.

## The questions, in one place

Before you buy something on the strength of a green claim, ask:

1. Does the claim name a **number** or just a feeling?
2. Can I check it against an **independent** source or certification?
3. Does it account for the product's **whole life**, not one stage?
4. Is it solving the **real** problem, or a visible small one?
5. Would **keeping what I already have** beat buying this at all?

Most claims fail two or three of these. That is fine. The goal is not to buy the
perfect thing. It is to stop paying a premium for a sticker, and to put your
money where the evidence actually is. The greenest purchase is usually the one
you talked yourself out of.
