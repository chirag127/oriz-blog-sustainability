The average household bins a quarter to a third of the food it buys. Not because
people are careless, but because kitchens are built around buying more, not
finishing what is there. You do not need bamboo cutlery or a chest freezer to
change that. You need a few habits and the willingness to look at your bin as
data.

Here is what actually moves the needle, roughly in order of effort-to-payoff.

## Shop your fridge before you shop the shop

The single biggest source of waste is buying food you already have. Before any
grocery run, spend two minutes looking at what is about to turn: the half bag of
spinach, the yoghurt three days from its date, the heel of a loaf. Plan one meal
around those first. A "use-it-up" shelf at eye level, where things on their way
out go, turns the vague guilt of a full fridge into a clear to-do list.

## Store things so they last

Most produce dies early from bad storage, not age.

- **Herbs**: treat them like flowers. Stems in a glass of water, a bag loosely
  over the top, in the fridge. Coriander goes from two days to two weeks.
- **Leafy greens**: wash, spin dry, wrap in a tea towel in a box. Moisture is
  what rots them.
- **Onions, garlic, potatoes**: cool, dark, dry, and never together. Potatoes
  next to onions sprout faster.
- **Bread**: freeze half the loaf the day you buy it. Toast straight from
  frozen. Bread going stale on the counter is pure avoidable loss.

None of this costs anything. It is just knowing which mistake you were making.

## Cook from scraps, not just ingredients

The parts you throw away are often the parts with the most flavour.

- Vegetable ends, onion skins, and herb stems go in a bag in the freezer. When
  it is full, simmer it into stock. Free, better than a cube, and it clears the
  freezer.
- Stale bread becomes croutons, breadcrumbs, or a next-day panzanella.
- Wilting vegetables are exactly what soup, frittata, and fried rice are for.
  "Fridge-bottom" as a recipe category is a skill worth building.

## Get honest about portions

Cooking too much and binning leftovers is waste that hides as generosity. Cook
what you will actually eat, and treat deliberate leftovers as tomorrow's lunch,
not an accident. A stack of labelled containers makes leftovers feel like a
planned meal instead of a chore.

## Compost what is truly left

After all that, some scraps remain: peels, coffee grounds, eggshells. These do
not belong in landfill, where they rot without oxygen and release methane. A
small countertop caddy emptied into a garden bin, a community scheme, or a
council food-waste collection closes the loop. If you have a garden, the compost
comes back as soil. If you do not, most cities now collect food waste separately;
check what your council offers before assuming you have no option.

## The one metric that matters

Look in your bin once a week. What is in there? A blackened bag of salad every
week means you keep buying salad you do not eat, so buy less. A carton of milk
every fortnight means you buy too big, so buy smaller. The bin is the most honest
feedback loop in your house. Read it, adjust, repeat.

Low-waste is not a purity test. It is a slow tightening of the gap between what
you buy and what you eat. Start with storage and the use-it-up shelf this week.
The gadgets can wait, probably forever.
